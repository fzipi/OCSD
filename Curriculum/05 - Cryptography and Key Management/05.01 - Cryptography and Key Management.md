# Module 5 – Cryptography and Key Management

This outline defines the cryptography and key management knowledge assessed in Module 5.

Transport Layer Security (TLS) configuration, cipher suite selection, and forward secrecy are assessed in Module 9, sections 9.3, 9.4, and 9.8.
Signed tokens such as JSON Web Tokens (JWT) are assessed in Module 2, section 2.5.
Password policy and credential recovery are assessed in Module 2, section 2.2.
Module 5 covers the cryptographic primitives and key management that those modules rely on.

## 5.1 Security properties of cryptography

- Confidentiality, integrity, authenticity, and non-repudiation as distinct properties
- Which mechanism supports each property: encryption, hash functions, message authentication codes (MACs), and digital signatures
- Non-repudiation as dependent on key control, identity proofing, and logging, not on cryptography alone
- Plaintext, ciphertext, keys, and algorithms as the basic terms of encryption
- The limits of cryptography: it does not replace authentication, authorization, or secure design, and it does not protect data after a legitimate recipient decrypts it
- The security of a cryptographic system depends on the secrecy of its keys, not on the secrecy of its algorithm

## 5.2 Encoding, encryption, and hashing

- Encoding as a reversible transformation for representation or transport that uses no secret
- Base64, hexadecimal, and URL encoding as encodings that provide no confidentiality
- Encryption as a reversible transformation that requires a key
- Hashing as a one-way transformation that produces a fixed-length digest
- Which of the three fits a stated need: representing data, keeping data secret, detecting changes, or storing a password
- Keys stored in Base64 as unprotected keys

## 5.3 Encryption

- Symmetric cryptography as one shared key for encryption and decryption, and its suitability for large amounts of data
- Asymmetric cryptography as a public and private key pair, and its use for key exchange and digital signatures
- Hybrid systems that use asymmetric cryptography to establish keys and symmetric cryptography to protect data
- AES and ChaCha20 as modern symmetric ciphers
- RSA and elliptic curve cryptography as widely used asymmetric families
- Encryption without integrity protection as vulnerable to undetected modification
- Authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD) as the preferred way to provide confidentiality and integrity together
- AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 as common AEAD constructions
- Electronic Codebook (ECB) mode as unsuitable because it reveals patterns in plaintext
- Cipher Block Chaining (CBC) mode as requiring separate integrity protection
- Nonces and initialization vectors (IVs): they need not be secret, they must not repeat under the same key, and some modes also require them to be unpredictable
- Use of well-reviewed libraries and high-level APIs in place of custom constructions
- Minimum key sizes as determined by current industry guidance

## 5.4 Hash functions, MACs, digital signatures, and certificates

- Properties of a cryptographic hash function: preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance, and collision resistance
- SHA-2 and SHA-3 as current hash families, and MD5 and SHA-1 as unsuitable for security purposes
- A hash alone as proof of integrity only if the hash itself is protected
- A MAC as integrity and authenticity protection for parties that share a secret key
- HMAC as the standard MAC construction built from a hash function
- Digital signatures as integrity and authenticity protection verified with a public key
- The difference between a MAC and a digital signature: anyone who can verify a MAC can also create one
- Verification of a signature or MAC before the signed data is used
- X.509 certificates as bindings between a public key and an identity
- Certificate authorities, certificate chains, and root trust stores
- Validity period, hostname, chain, and revocation as parts of certificate validation
- Revocation through certificate revocation lists (CRLs) and the Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP)

## 5.5 Password hashing and random numbers

- Password hashing as a distinct use of hashing that must be slow and expensive for an attacker
- Argon2id, scrypt, and bcrypt as dedicated password-hashing algorithms
- General-purpose hashes such as SHA-256 as unsuitable for password storage
- Salts as unique, random, non-secret values that defeat precomputed tables and hide duplicate passwords
- Work-factor parameters that are reviewed and increased as hardware improves
- Password hashing as protection of stored passwords, and Module 2 as the owner of password policy
- Cryptographically secure random number generators (CSPRNGs) as required for keys, nonces, salts, and security tokens
- Predictability of general-purpose pseudo-random number generators such as `Math.random()`
- Operating system and framework CSPRNGs as the preferred source of randomness
- Insufficient entropy as a cause of guessable keys and tokens

## 5.6 Weaknesses and attacks

- Weak and deprecated algorithms and key sizes, and the need to follow current guidance
- Hardcoded keys and secrets in source code, repositories, and client applications
- Custom cryptography and custom protocol designs as high-risk
- Nonce or IV reuse and its consequences
- Disabled or incomplete certificate validation
- Downgrade attacks that force weaker protocol versions or algorithms
- Brute-force and dictionary attacks, and the role of key length, entropy, and hashing cost
- Rainbow table attacks and the defense provided by salts
- Hash collision attacks and the reason MD5 and SHA-1 are unsuitable
- Length extension attacks and the reason to use HMAC instead of `hash(secret || message)`
- Replay attacks and defenses such as nonces, timestamps, and short lifetimes
- Man-in-the-middle attacks and the dependence of defenses on correct identity verification
- Padding oracle attacks and the role of authenticated encryption in preventing them
- Timing side channels and the use of constant-time comparison for secrets
- Key theft as a more common cause of failure than algorithm weakness

## 5.7 Key management

- The key lifecycle: generation, distribution, storage, use, rotation, revocation, and destruction
- Key generation with a CSPRNG or an approved library, and not from predictable or user-chosen values
- Separate keys for separate purposes
- Storage in hardware security modules (HSMs), cloud key management services (KMS), secrets managers, or operating system key stores
- Keys stored separately from the data they protect
- Source code, repositories, logs, and unprotected backups as unsuitable places for keys
- Least-privilege access to keys, and logging of key use
- Key exchange and distribution through authenticated channels, not through email or chat
- Key rotation on a defined schedule, after suspected compromise, and when guidance changes
- Key versioning that allows data encrypted under an old key to be decrypted during rotation
- Re-encryption of data after rotation of a symmetric key
- Key destruction and the loss of access to data that results from deleting a key
- Compromised keys as requiring revocation and replacement
- Module 14 as the owner of secrets management in deployment
