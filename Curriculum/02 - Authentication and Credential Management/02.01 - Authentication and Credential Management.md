# Module 2 - Authentication and Credential Management

## 2.1 Authentication foundations

- The distinction among identification, authentication, identity proofing, and authorization
- The relationship among an identity, an identifier, an authenticator, a credential, and a session
- Human identities and non-human identities, including services, devices, scheduled jobs, and deployment processes
- The distinction between an entity and the identity that represents it
- The knowledge, possession, and inherence factor categories
- The difference between authentication factors and risk signals such as location, device reputation, and behavior
- Authentication methods and their suitability for different subjects, threats, and required assurance
- Equivalent authentication strength across web pages, APIs, mobile clients, administrative paths, and recovery flows
- The use of established identity services and framework features, and the application team's responsibility for configuration and validation
- Authorization as a separate decision covered in Module 3 (section 3.1)

## 2.2 Credential lifecycle management

- Credential provisioning, delivery, storage, rotation, revocation, recovery, and retirement
- Password policy that favors length, password managers, and checks against common and breached passwords
- Composition rules and routine password changes as practices that do not improve security without evidence of compromise
- Password storage with a purpose-built password-hashing algorithm, a unique salt, and a work factor, with the algorithms owned by Module 5
- The difference between password hashing, reversible encryption, and fast general-purpose hashes
- Recovery flows and authenticator changes as alternative routes around the normal login method
- Single-use, short-lived recovery tokens and notification of changes through an existing trusted channel
- Credentials for non-human identities: API keys, client secrets, service-account credentials, certificates, and platform-managed identities
- Short-lived and managed credentials compared with long-lived shared secrets
- Locations where credentials do not belong, such as source code, container images, URLs, logs, and client-side bundles
- Account enumeration, brute force, password spraying, and credential stuffing, and the availability effects of account lockout

## 2.3 Multi-factor and passwordless authentication

- The security properties and limitations of SMS codes, time-based one-time passwords (TOTP), push approval, security keys, and backup codes
- Phishing, SIM swapping, real-time relay, and authentication fatigue as attacks against multi-factor authentication (MFA)
- Passkeys, FIDO2, and Web Authentication (WebAuthn) as origin-bound public-key authentication
- The difference between synchronized passkeys and device-bound credentials, and what an application can verify about them
- Passwordless methods such as passkeys, email sign-in links, and one-time codes, and the bearer-credential risks of the latter two
- Step-up authentication and recent authentication before changing authentication factors or performing sensitive operations
- Enrollment, replacement, fallback, and recovery paths as limits on the strength of MFA

## 2.4 Authenticated sessions

- The purpose of a session in a stateless protocol and the risk of a stolen bearer session token
- Reference tokens and self-contained tokens, and the difference between token design and token transport
- Unpredictable session identifiers and protection of tokens in transport, storage, URLs, error messages, and logs
- The `Secure`, `HttpOnly`, `SameSite`, `Domain`, and `Path` cookie attributes
- The `__Secure-` and `__Host-` cookie prefixes and the restrictions they enforce
- The risk of storing bearer tokens in script-accessible storage
- Session creation, renewal after authentication and privilege changes, idle timeout, absolute lifetime, and concurrent sessions
- Server-side enforcement of timeouts, logout, and termination after account disablement or credential compromise
- Re-authentication before sensitive operations
- Session fixation and session hijacking, and the controls that reduce them
- Cache controls that keep authenticated content out of shared and browser caches and out of history after logout
- Transport protection and browser controls for cookies as covered in Module 9 (section 9.6)

## 2.5 Cross-site request forgery

- Cross-site request forgery (CSRF) as a request that a browser sends with the victim's cookies from another site
- Why cookie-based sessions, HTTP authentication, and client certificates are exposed to CSRF and why explicit authorization headers are not
- State-changing requests as the target, and the effect of using safe HTTP methods only for reads
- `SameSite=Lax` and `SameSite=Strict` as a defense in depth, and their limits for same-site and top-level navigation requests
- Synchronizer tokens and signed double-submit tokens as request-specific proofs
- Validation of the `Origin` and `Referer` headers and Fetch metadata request headers
- Custom request headers and the reasons they are not a standalone defense
- Cross-site scripting as a way to defeat CSRF defenses
- Authentication callbacks and login requests as CSRF targets, including the OAuth and OIDC `state` value

## 2.6 Federation, single sign-on, and self-contained tokens

- Single sign-on (SSO) as a user experience and federation as trust in another security domain's identity information
- Identity providers and relying parties or service providers
- The distinction among OAuth 2.0 for delegated authorization, OpenID Connect (OIDC) for authentication, and Security Assertion Markup Language (SAML) for signed assertions
- Authorization Code flow with an exact redirect URI, `state`, `nonce`, and Proof Key for Code Exchange (PKCE)
- Validation of SAML assertions: signature, issuer, audience, recipient, and validity conditions
- Trusted configuration of expected issuers, endpoints, algorithms, and keys, and untrusted token values that must not choose them
- Self-contained tokens, including the JSON Web Token (JWT) format, as signed or encrypted claims
- The distinction between encoding, signing, and encryption of a token
- Signature verification with an explicitly allowed algorithm, the `alg: none` and algorithm-confusion attacks, and key selection by `kid` from a trusted key set
- Validation of `iss`, `aud`, `exp`, `nbf`, token type, and flow-specific claims such as `nonce`
- Short token lifetimes and revocation designs for self-contained tokens, including deny lists, token identifiers, and key rotation
- The different purposes of ID tokens, access tokens, refresh tokens, and application session tokens
- The exclusion of secrets and unnecessary personal information from readable token claims
- JWT use in APIs as related to Module 13 (section 13.2), and signing algorithms as owned by Module 5

## 2.7 Authentication failures and testing

- Common authentication and session failures, including those in registration, recovery, MFA, SSO callbacks, remember-me features, and support tools
- Test cases for valid, missing, incorrect, expired, revoked, replayed, and malformed credentials and tokens
- Test cases for password reset, factor replacement, fallback, and recovery
- Test cases for session creation, renewal, timeout, logout, revocation, and fixation
- Test cases for CSRF protection on state-changing requests
- Test cases for federated login with invalid signatures, issuers, audiences, redirect URIs, `state`, `nonce`, and validity periods
- Direct requests to protected endpoints that bypass the user interface
- Rate limiting, account enumeration, and the absence of credentials from logs and error responses
- Positive and negative tests as part of a regression suite
