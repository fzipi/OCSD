# Module 9 - Secure Communications

This outline defines the secure communications knowledge assessed in Module 9.

## 9.1 Transport security basics

- Confidentiality, integrity, and endpoint authentication as the properties that TLS provides
- Why TLS does not replace application authentication or authorization
- Why TLS does not protect data once it reaches an endpoint, including logs, caches, and storage
- The difference between an eavesdropper and an attacker who modifies traffic (man-in-the-middle)
- Why maintained TLS libraries, frameworks, and managed services are preferred over custom transport security
- TLS 1.3 and TLS 1.2 as the versions in current use, and SSL and TLS 1.0 and 1.1 as obsolete
- Why default settings of current libraries are preferred over hand-selected cipher suites
- The difference between a strong, current configuration and obsolete options such as NULL, export-grade, and RC4 ciphers
- Forward secrecy as protection of recorded past sessions after a private key is compromised
- The relationship between forward secrecy and ephemeral key exchange

## 9.2 Certificates and trust

- A certificate as a binding between an identity and a public key, vouched for by a certificate authority (CA)
- The three checks of a client: a chain to a trusted root, a hostname match, and a valid time period
- Typical causes of validation failures: expired certificates, missing intermediate certificates, hostname mismatch, and untrusted issuers
- Why disabling certificate or hostname verification defeats the purpose of TLS, even for testing or internal services
- Responses that keep verification: fixing the certificate chain, renewing the certificate, or adding the correct private CA to the trust store
- Certificate lifecycle: issuance, renewal, rotation, and revocation
- Replacement of the certificate and key pair, and revocation, after a private key is compromised
- Protection of private keys and appropriate certificate scope, including the risk of wildcard certificates
- Certificate Transparency logs as a way to detect unexpected certificates issued for a domain
- DNS CAA records as a way to restrict which CAs may issue certificates for a domain
- Certificate or public-key pinning as a control for non-browser clients, such as mobile apps and API clients
- Risks of pinning: outages after certificate rotation, and the need for backup pins and a planned update path
- Why pin mismatches are treated as connection failures rather than bypassed
- Why browser-based public-key pinning (HPKP) is obsolete

## 9.3 Enforcing HTTPS in browsers

- HTTPS redirection and why the first request over HTTP can still be intercepted
- HSTS as an instruction to browsers to use only HTTPS for a host for the period set by `max-age`
- The effect of `includeSubDomains` and the need to confirm that all subdomains support HTTPS first
- HSTS preloading and why removal from a preload list is slow
- The first-connection limitation of HSTS without preloading
- Mixed content as an HTTPS page loading resources over HTTP, and the difference between passive and active mixed content
- The CSP directive `upgrade-insecure-requests` as a way to request HTTPS for resources the page loads over HTTP
- The CSP directive `connect-src` as a restriction of destinations for scripted connections such as `fetch` and WebSocket
- Why these controls do not replace TLS, HSTS, or server-side authorization
- The `Secure` cookie attribute as a restriction to HTTPS transmission, and its limits (it does not encrypt the value and does not stop script access or cross-site requests)
- Other cookie attributes and cross-site request forgery (CSRF) as topics owned by Module 2 (section 2.4 for cookies and 2.5 for CSRF)

## 9.4 Connections between components

- Connections between client, proxy, application, and external services as separate links that each need protection
- TLS termination at a load balancer or reverse proxy, and the plaintext segment behind it
- Re-encryption or a verified private network as responses to plaintext segments
- Unsafe fallback to plaintext, such as optional TLS or HTTP downgrade when an HTTPS connection fails
- Trust in forwarded headers about the original protocol and client address only from known proxies
- Outbound connections from an application to external services as clients that must verify certificates
- Server-authenticated TLS versus mutual TLS (mTLS), and which side presents a certificate in each
- Certificate identities from mTLS as input to access decisions
- Why a verified client certificate proves identity but does not grant authorization
- Typical mTLS uses: service-to-service calls, API clients, and device authentication

## 9.5 WebSockets

- WebSocket as a long-lived two-way connection started by an HTTP request
- `ws://` versus `wss://`, and `wss://` as the form protected by TLS
- Cross-Site WebSocket Hijacking when a handshake is authenticated by cookies only
- Why the same-origin policy does not block cross-origin WebSocket connections
- Validation of the `Origin` header during the handshake
- Authentication of the connection and authorization of individual messages or actions
- Validation of message content as untrusted input
- Limits on message size, message rate, number of connections, and idle time
- Termination of the connection when the session ends or credentials are revoked

## 9.6 Browser security model

- Origin as the combination of scheme, host, and port
- The same-origin policy as a restriction on scripts reading responses from other origins
- The fact that the same-origin policy does not stop a browser from sending a cross-origin request
- CORS as a server-controlled relaxation of the same-origin policy, using headers such as `Access-Control-Allow-Origin`
- Why CORS is not an access-control mechanism for the server, and why it does not stop CSRF
- Risky CORS configurations: reflecting any `Origin`, allowing the value `null`, and allowing credentials for untrusted origins
- Content Security Policy as a browser-enforced restriction on the sources of scripts and other resources, used as defense in depth against cross-site scripting (see Module 4, section 4.4)
- Clickjacking as a user being tricked into interacting with a hidden framed page
- `frame-ancestors` in CSP as the control for which origins may frame a page, and `X-Frame-Options` as its older equivalent
- `X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff` as a control against content type guessing by the browser
- `Referrer-Policy` as a control over which URL information is sent to other sites
- Security headers as defense in depth that supplements, and does not replace, secure code
