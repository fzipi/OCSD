# Objectives for Module 9 - Secure Communications

This module covers how web applications protect data in transit between clients, proxies, and services.
It also covers the browser security controls that decide how web content may interact with other origins.

A successful candidate can:

- Identify what Transport Layer Security (TLS) protects and what it does not protect
- Recognize recommended and obsolete TLS versions without needing to select individual cipher suites
- Distinguish forward secrecy from the protection that certificate authentication provides
- Recognize certificate-validation failures involving the trust chain, hostname, or validity period
- Identify responses to validation failures that keep verification of the intended endpoint
- Recognize certificate and private-key lifecycle requirements, including renewal and replacement after key compromise
- Distinguish certificate pinning in non-browser clients from alternatives such as DNS CAA records
- Distinguish HTTPS redirection from HSTS, including `max-age`, `includeSubDomains`, preloading, and the first-connection limitation
- Identify mixed content and recognize the role of the CSP directives `upgrade-insecure-requests` and `connect-src`
- Recognize the purpose and limits of the `Secure` cookie attribute
- Identify connections that remain unprotected after TLS termination or through fallback to plaintext
- Distinguish server-authenticated TLS from mutual TLS (mTLS), and authentication from authorization
- Recognize WebSocket risks that remain when `wss://` is used
- Distinguish the same-origin policy from cross-origin resource sharing (CORS)
- Recognize the purpose of Content Security Policy (CSP), `frame-ancestors`, and common security response headers

## Expected competency

Candidates can distinguish secure from insecure communication choices in ordinary web development scenarios.
For example, given a web application behind a reverse proxy that calls an external API, they can identify an unprotected connection or an invalid certificate and recognize a response that maintains security.
They can also identify whether a browser-side control, such as CORS or CSP, addresses a described risk.
