# Supplementary reading - Data Protection and Privacy

This chapter covers how developers classify, protect, minimize, pseudonymize, and delete sensitive and personal data.

Data protection keeps information confidential, accurate, and available only to those who need it.
Privacy is the related goal that individuals keep meaningful control over information about themselves.
Both are engineering requirements.
They shape data models, APIs, logs, and backups, and they are expensive to add after a system is built.
A system that handles data carelessly can harm real people in ways a later patch cannot undo.

## 8.1 Data classification and inventory

Data classification labels information by its sensitivity and the harm caused by its disclosure, modification, or loss.
Without labels, teams either under-protect sensitive data or spend effort protecting data that does not need it.

Organizations name the levels differently.
A common four-level model is:

- Public data is meant for anyone, such as published documentation.
  Protect its integrity so that nobody can alter it.
- Internal data is for use inside the organization, such as process documents and operational metrics.
  Require authentication and keep it off public channels.
- Confidential data could cause real harm if disclosed, such as customer records, contracts, and non-public financial data.
  Restrict access to those who need it, encrypt it, and log access.
- Restricted data would cause serious harm if exposed, such as credentials, cryptographic keys, payment card data, health records, and government identifiers.
  Apply the strictest access limits, field-level protection, and detailed audit records.

Classification matters only when it drives controls.
Each level should map to requirements for access, encryption, storage location, sharing with third parties, retention, and disposal.
Controls that the platform enforces, such as a policy that rejects unencrypted storage for data tagged restricted, are more reliable than rules each developer must remember.

### Types of sensitive data

Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person.
Examples are names, email addresses, phone numbers, location data, and online identifiers.
Some personal data is more sensitive, such as health data, biometric data, and government identifiers.
Financial data, credentials, and cryptographic keys are sensitive whether or not they relate to a person.

### Knowing where data lives

You cannot protect data you do not know you have.
Sensitive data spreads beyond the main database into:

- Logs, caches, and message queues
- Backups and database replicas
- Search indexes and analytics pipelines
- File uploads and data exports
- Test and development environments

A data inventory records what data the application holds, why, who can access it, and how long it is kept.
A data flow map shows how data enters, moves through, and leaves the system, for example from browser to API, API to database, and application to a third-party processor.
Data flow maps support threat modeling (see Module 1, section 1.3), incident response, and legal duties such as records of processing under GDPR.
An outdated map is risky because it gives false confidence.

Automated data discovery tools scan databases, object stores, and repositories for patterns such as card numbers or national identifiers.
They find forgotten copies, but they produce false positives and miss data in unusual formats.
They support, and do not replace, a deliberate review of the design.

### Risks of improper protection

Weak data protection leads to leakage through logs, backups, or over-broad APIs, and to unauthorized access.
It can also lead to regulatory penalties and loss of user trust.
The harm to individuals, such as fraud, discrimination, or exposure of private life, is often the most serious consequence.
[OWASP Top 10:2025 A04—Cryptographic Failures](https://top10.owasp.org/2025/A04_2025-Cryptographic_Failures/) describes how missing or weak protection of sensitive data leads to exposure.

## 8.2 Protecting stored data and data in transit

Encryption at rest keeps stored data unreadable without the key.
It protects against theft of a disk, a copied backup, or an exposed storage bucket.
It is not a complete control.
If an attacker controls the running application, or can run authorized database queries, the data is decrypted for them.

### Encryption layers

Different layers address different threats:

- Full disk encryption protects a whole volume.
  It helps if hardware or a disk snapshot is stolen.
  Once the system is running, the volume is mounted and readable by anything with access.
- Database encryption, often called transparent data encryption (TDE), encrypts database files and logs.
  It protects the storage and backups and needs few application changes.
  It does not stop SQL injection or a compromised application account, because the database decrypts the data before returning it.
- File-level encryption protects individual files or objects, such as exports and uploaded documents, and can use separate keys for each.
- Field-level encryption protects specific values, such as a national identifier or bank account number, inside a record.
  The application encrypts and decrypts, so even someone with full database access cannot read those fields without the key.
  The cost is that the database cannot index, sort, or search the encrypted values directly.

Choose the layer from the threat you want to stop.
Disk and database encryption address stolen storage.
Field-level encryption also reduces exposure to database administrators and injection flaws.

### Keys and separation

Encryption is only as strong as the protection of its keys.
Do not store a key beside the data it protects.
A database key kept in the same database, or in a configuration file on the same server, gives no protection when that server is compromised.
Keep keys in a hardware security module (HSM) or a managed key service, and let applications request cryptographic operations instead of reading the key.
Key lifecycle topics such as generation, rotation, and revocation belong to Module 5 (see section 5.6).
The [OWASP Key Management Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Key_Management_Cheat_Sheet.html) and the [OWASP Cryptographic Storage Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Cryptographic_Storage_Cheat_Sheet.html) cover them in more depth.

Envelope encryption makes rotation practical for large data sets.
Each record or file is encrypted with its own data encryption key (DEK).
A key encryption key (KEK) in the key service encrypts the DEKs.
Rotating the KEK means re-encrypting only the small DEKs, not all the data.

### Practical effects

Encryption affects how the application works:

- Searching: encrypted fields cannot be searched with `LIKE` or range queries.
  Keep a separate non-sensitive field when you need lookups, such as the last four digits of a card number.
  Deterministic encryption allows equality matching but reveals which records hold the same value.
- Backups: a plaintext backup of an encrypted database defeats the encryption.
  Encrypt backups, and make sure the keys needed for recovery survive the disaster the backup protects against.
- Performance: measure the cost in your own workload.
  Disk and database encryption usually add little overhead on modern hardware.
  Field-level encryption in the application adds more.

### Data in transit

Sensitive data is also exposed while it moves between browser and server, between services, and to third parties.
Use TLS for every connection that carries sensitive data, including internal service traffic.
TLS configuration is covered in Module 9 (see sections 9.1 and 9.3).
The [OWASP Transport Layer Security Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Transport_Layer_Security_Cheat_Sheet.html) lists recommended settings.

### Unintended exposure

Data is often exposed by the application itself rather than by the storage layer:

- URLs and query strings are stored in browser history, server logs, proxy logs, and referrer headers.
  Do not put session identifiers, tokens, or personal data in them.
- Logs and error messages can record request bodies, credentials, or personal data (see Module 7, section 7.2).
- Caches, including shared proxy caches and content delivery networks, can keep and serve responses that contain personal data unless the response is marked as non-cacheable.
- Client-side storage such as `localStorage` is readable by any script running on the page, so a single cross-site scripting flaw exposes everything kept there.
  See the [OWASP HTML5 Security Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/HTML5_Security_Cheat_Sheet.html).
  The [MDN Web Storage API documentation](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Storage_API) explains how these stores behave.

## 8.3 Data minimization and retention

Data minimization means collecting, using, and keeping only the data needed for a defined purpose.
It is both a privacy principle and a security control.
Data you do not hold cannot be stolen, subpoenaed, or misused.

### Collection and purpose

Before adding a form field, a database column, or an API parameter, ask what function it supports.
If nobody can answer, do not collect it.
Prefer less precise data when it meets the need, such as an age range or a year of birth instead of a full birth date.

Purpose limitation means data collected for one purpose is not reused for an unrelated one without a lawful basis.
For example, delivery addresses collected to ship an order should not feed a marketing profile without separate disclosure and, where required, consent.
In design terms, keep data for different purposes in separate stores with separate access rules.

### Fewer copies and smaller responses

Every copy of sensitive data is another place it can leak.
Copies appear as replicas, exports for analysts, test databases, caches, and files sent to partners.
Track them and protect them as strongly as the original.

APIs should return only the fields each client needs.
A navigation bar that shows a name and avatar does not need an address or phone number.
Use explicit response schemas per endpoint and avoid returning whole database objects.
Over-broad responses also appear when frameworks bind request data directly to internal objects; see the [OWASP Mass Assignment Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Mass_Assignment_Cheat_Sheet.html).

Logs should record what happened, not the personal data involved.
Log that a user updated a profile field, not the old and new values.
The [OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Logging_Cheat_Sheet.html) lists data that should be excluded or masked.

### Retention

A retention period states how long each kind of data is kept and why.
Base it on business need and legal obligations.
"We delete it when we no longer need it" is not a policy.
"Support tickets are deleted three years after closing" is a policy that code can enforce.

Enforce retention automatically, for example with scheduled jobs or storage lifecycle rules.
Manual cleanup does not scale and is often forgotten.
Design the schema so data with different retention periods can be deleted independently, for example by keeping optional sensitive fields in a separate table.
Module 7 covers log protection (see section 7.4), and the rules in this chapter apply to the personal data they contain.

## 8.4 Anonymization and pseudonymization

Sometimes data must be used for analytics, testing, or research without identifying individuals.
Two approaches reduce risk, and they are not the same.

- Anonymization removes the link between data and a person so that re-identification is not reasonably possible.
  Properly anonymized data is generally no longer personal data under laws such as GDPR.
- Pseudonymization replaces identifiers with substitutes, but the person can still be re-identified using additional information kept separately.
  Pseudonymized data is still personal data.

### Anonymization is hard

Removing names and ID numbers is not enough.
Quasi-identifiers such as birth date, postal code, gender, or occupation can be combined with other data sets to identify people.
Researchers have re-identified people in published data sets that had direct identifiers removed.
Do not call a data set anonymous until you have considered what other data an attacker could combine with it.

### Anonymization techniques

- Masking replaces or hides part of a value, such as showing only the last four digits of an account number.
  Masking helps in displays and test data, but a masked data set may still be re-identifiable.
- Generalization reduces precision, such as an age range instead of an exact age or a region instead of an address.
- Swapping exchanges values between records, so each value is real but not attached to the right person.
  Overall distributions are preserved.
- Noise addition perturbs numerical values or aggregate results by a small random amount.
  The noise must be large enough to protect individuals and small enough to keep the data useful.

### Pseudonymization techniques

- Tokenization replaces a value with a random token.
  A separate, tightly controlled token vault maps tokens back to the original value.
  Because tokens are random, nothing can be recovered from the token without the vault.
  Payment systems use it to keep card numbers out of most of the environment.
- Keyed hashing computes an HMAC (a hash combined with a secret key, defined in [RFC 2104](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc2104)) of the identifier.
  The same input and key always give the same pseudonym, so records can still be linked.
  Store the key separately from the pseudonymized data, because anyone with the key can recompute pseudonyms for guessed values.
- Encryption-based pseudonymization encrypts the identifier so authorized parties can reverse it with the key.

### Why a salted hash is not a safe pseudonym

A plain or salted hash looks like a pseudonym, but it often is not.
The outcome depends on how the salt is used:

- With a unique random salt per record, the same email address produces different hashes.
  The data can no longer be linked across records, so the hash is not useful as a stable pseudonym.
- With one constant salt for the whole data set, the same email address always gives the same hash, so linking works.
  But identifiers such as emails, phone numbers, and national ID numbers come from small or predictable sets.
  An attacker who has the data and the salt, which is usually stored alongside it, can hash every candidate value and compare.
  This enumeration recovers the original identifiers.
- A salt is not a secret.
  It defeats precomputed tables, not guessing.

For linkable pseudonyms, use an HMAC with a secret key stored separately, or use tokenization.
For identifiers that need no linking, drop them or replace them with random values.
Even then, the result is pseudonymized data, not anonymized data, as long as the key or vault exists.
Hashing passwords is a different problem and is covered in Module 2 and Module 5.

### Reversible and irreversible

Tokenization with a vault and encryption-based pseudonymization are reversible.
They require protection of the vault or key and keep the data within privacy law.
Techniques such as generalization and noise addition are intended to be irreversible, but only a re-identification risk assessment shows whether they succeed.

### Test environments

Real production data in test and development environments is a common cause of breaches.
Those environments usually have weaker access control, more users, and less monitoring.
Better options are:

- Synthetic data, generated to match the structure and statistics of real data without containing real records.
  For relational schemas it must preserve relationships and realistic value ranges.
- Masked or anonymized copies, produced by an automated and repeatable process rather than a one-time manual export.
- A small subset of data that covers the cases the tests need, which also limits exposure.

The [OWASP Privacy Toolkit](https://owasp.org/projects/privacy-toolkit) project collects privacy engineering resources.

## 8.5 Secure data deletion

Deleting a row does not delete data.
Copies may remain in backups, caches, replicas, queues, logs, analytics stores, and the systems of third parties.
When data must be deleted, because a retention period ended, a contract ended, or an individual requested it, the deletion must be effective and verifiable.

### Deletion methods

- Cryptographic erasure destroys the key that decrypts the data.
  Data encrypted with that key becomes unreadable even though ciphertext remains.
  This is the primary method for modern storage.
  It is fast, works across replicas and backups that share the key, and does not depend on the storage hardware.
  It only works if the data was encrypted from the start and no other copy of the key exists.
  Using separate keys for each user or tenant allows deleting one person's data without affecting others.
  NIST describes it in the glossary entry on [cryptographic erase](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/cryptographic_erase).
- Overwriting writes new data over the old contents.
  It was designed for magnetic hard drives.
  It is unreliable on solid-state drives, because wear leveling and spare blocks mean the drive may keep the original data in places the operating system cannot reach.
  It is also unreliable on cloud storage, where you do not control which physical device holds each copy.
  Do not rely on it for these.
- Physical destruction, such as shredding, applies to retired hardware.
  It is for the organization that owns the media, and it is not available to application developers using cloud services.

[NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 1, Guidelines for Media Sanitization](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/88/r1/final) describes these methods and when each is appropriate.

In cloud environments, understand what the provider's deletion guarantees are and keep evidence of deletion.
Using customer-managed keys in a key management service gives you deletion control through key destruction, independent of how the provider manages physical storage.

### Where deleted data persists

A deletion design must account for:

- Backups, which keep data until they expire.
  Define backup retention to match data retention, and document how erasure requests are handled for backups, for example by letting them expire or by using per-record keys.
- Caches, search indexes, read replicas, content delivery networks, and materialized views.
- Offline copies on user devices where the application stores data locally.
- Third parties that received the data.
  Contracts should require them to delete it, and the application should send deletion requests to them.
- Legal holds, which may require keeping data despite a deletion request.

### Deleting a user's data

Data about one person usually spans many tables and systems: the profile, orders, support tickets, analytics events, and logs.
Deletion needs a complete data map.
Cascading deletes in the data model, such as foreign keys with `ON DELETE CASCADE`, handle records in one database.
Application-level workflows handle the rest.

A deletion request workflow should:

- Authenticate the requester before any data is removed or exported.
- Track the request through completion against the time limit that applies.
- Verify the result by checking each data store for remaining records.
- Confirm the outcome to the requester.

Keep an audit record of the deletion.
It should hold only metadata, such as a record identifier, the time, who or what initiated it, and the outcome.
It must not recreate the deleted data, and it should be protected against modification.

## 8.6 Privacy by design and by default

Privacy by design means considering privacy during design rather than after release.
Dr. Ann Cavoukian formulated it as seven foundational principles.
Those are proactive rather than reactive, privacy as the default, privacy embedded into design, full functionality without trade-offs, end-to-end security, visibility and transparency, and respect for user privacy.
You do not need to recite them.
You do need to recognize what they mean in practice.
GDPR Article 25 makes data protection by design and by default a legal requirement, and the [European Data Protection Board guidelines on Article 25](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/documents/guideline/guidelines-42019-on-article-25-data-protection-by-design-and-by-default_en) explain it.

In practice:

- Proactive means finding privacy risks in design reviews and threat modeling, before the feature ships.
- By default means a user who changes nothing gets the most protective settings: the least data shared, the shortest retention, and the narrowest visibility.
  Users opt in to sharing more.
- Embedded means privacy controls live in the architecture, such as the data model, the authorization layer, and logging, not in add-on features.
- End-to-end means data is protected through its whole lifecycle: collection, use, storage, sharing, and deletion.
  Encrypting stored data but sending it over plain HTTP, or protecting production while copying it to an open test database, breaks this.
- Transparency means users can understand what is collected and why, and the team can show how data flows internally.

### Privacy impact assessments

A privacy impact assessment (PIA), also called a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) under GDPR, is a structured review done before building a feature or system.
It asks what personal data is collected and why, how it is stored and protected, who can access it, how long it is kept, and what harm to individuals could result from misuse.
It leads to controls that go into the design.
GDPR requires one for processing likely to result in high risk to individuals.
Developers contribute accurate information about data flows, storage, and sharing.

### Consent and preferences

Where consent is the legal basis, the system needs a consent record.
It should capture who consented, to what, when, how, which version of the notice they saw, and when they withdrew.
Consent records are personal data too.

Preferences are only useful if they are enforced.
An opt-out setting that nothing checks is a privacy failure.
Put consent and preference checks in the code paths they control, such as the job that sends marketing email or the pipeline that feeds analytics, and implement them as shared functions rather than repeating them.
Withdrawal must reach every system that relies on the consent.

The [OWASP Top 10 Privacy Risks](https://owasp.org/projects/top-10-privacy-risks) project and the [OWASP User Privacy Protection Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/User_Privacy_Protection_Cheat_Sheet.html) list common privacy failures.

## 8.7 Privacy law and technical controls

Developers are not lawyers, but laws turn into requirements that developers build.
Which laws apply depends on where the individuals are, where the organization operates, and which sector it works in.
Ask the organization's legal or privacy team which rules apply, and treat their answer as the source for requirements.

Examples of laws and standards:

- The [General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) applies to processing personal data of people in the EU, wherever the organization is located.
  The UK GDPR is the equivalent regime in the United Kingdom.
- The [California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa), as amended by CPRA, gives California residents rights to know, delete, correct, and opt out of the sale or sharing of their personal information.
- Brazil's LGPD is a general data protection law with similar principles.
- HIPAA, a United States law, covers protected health information held by healthcare providers, health plans, and their business associates.
- The [Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)](https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/document_library/) is a contractual standard for organizations that store, process, or transmit payment card data.
  Tokenization is a common way to reduce the systems that fall in its scope.

### GDPR concepts

These terms appear in many privacy laws:

- Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person ([Article 4](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/)).
- A data subject is that person.
- Processing is any operation on personal data, including storing, reading, sharing, and deleting it.
- A controller decides why and how personal data is processed.
  A processor handles it on the controller's behalf, such as a cloud or email provider.

Processing needs a lawful basis ([Article 6](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/)).
The six bases are consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, and legitimate interests.
Legitimate interests requires a balancing test of the organization's interest against the individual's rights.
The basis affects design: for example, withdrawing consent must stop processing that relied on it, but a legal obligation to keep records may not allow deletion.

The principles in [Article 5](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/) and their technical meaning are:

- Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency: document and explain data uses.
- Purpose limitation: separate stores and access rules by purpose.
- Data minimization: collect and return fewer fields.
- Accuracy: allow corrections and propagate them.
- Storage limitation: enforce retention.
- Integrity and confidentiality: apply the security controls in this and other modules.
- Accountability: keep records and audit logs that show compliance.

### Individual rights

Laws give individuals rights that need workflows in the application:

- Access: retrieve all personal data held about a verified person across systems.
- Rectification: correct inaccurate data and propagate the correction.
- Erasure: delete the data, within legal limits such as a duty to keep records (see section 8.5 and [Article 17](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/)).
- Restriction and objection: stop or limit certain processing.
- Portability: export data in a structured, machine-readable form such as JSON or CSV.
- Safeguards against solely automated decisions with significant effects, such as human review.

Fulfilling these requires a data inventory.
A system that does not know where a person's data lives cannot answer an access or erasure request.
Requests must be authenticated, otherwise the workflow itself becomes a way to steal or delete another person's data.

### Technical controls behind legal duties

- Access control limited by role and need, applied at field level where needed (see Module 3 and the [OWASP Authorization Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Authorization_Cheat_Sheet.html)).
- Audit logging of access to, changes of, and exports of sensitive data, so the organization can show compliance and investigate incidents (see Module 7).
- Security of processing: [Article 32](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-32-gdpr/) requires appropriate technical measures, naming encryption and pseudonymization as examples.
- Breach detection and response: [Article 33](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-33-gdpr/) requires notifying the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours.
  Detection and logging make that possible.
- Cross-border transfers: [Chapter V of GDPR](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-44-gdpr/) restricts transfers of personal data outside the EU unless safeguards exist.
  This affects cloud region selection, replication, backup location, and the location of third-party processors.

## 8.8 Verifying data protection controls

Controls that exist in a design document may not work in the running system.
Verification checks the actual behavior.
This chapter limits itself to data-specific checks.
Module 14 covers configuration and deployment review.

### Checks to perform

- Sensitive data leakage: use realistic inputs and inspect logs, error messages, API responses, URLs, browser storage, analytics payloads, exports, and test data for passwords, tokens, personal data, or payment data.
- Encryption: read the data directly from the database or storage, bypassing the application, and confirm that fields meant to be encrypted are not plaintext.
  Capture test traffic and confirm sensitive data is not sent in cleartext.
  Confirm that backups are encrypted and keys are stored elsewhere.
- Access control: confirm that one user cannot read another's data by changing an identifier (see the [OWASP Insecure Direct Object Reference Prevention Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Insecure_Direct_Object_Reference_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html)).
  Confirm that lower-privilege roles cannot get restricted data through bulk exports or direct API calls, and that tenants cannot see each other's data.
- Error handling: confirm that error messages do not reveal personal data or internal details (see the [OWASP Error Handling Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Error_Handling_Cheat_Sheet.html)).
- Deletion: run a deletion request in a test environment that resembles production, then check every store, including replicas, caches, search indexes, and analytics, for the deleted data.
  Record which stores were checked and the result.

The [OWASP Web Security Testing Guide](https://wstg.owasp.org/v4.2/) describes test procedures for many of these checks.

### Automated scans and audits

Automated scanners can find plaintext secrets, storage that is open to the public, and personal data in logs.
Periodic audits review whether retention jobs run, deletion workflows complete, and privacy settings take effect.
Both find only what they are configured to look for.
Independent review by someone other than the implementing team catches different problems.

## Worked example: pseudonymizing customer email addresses for analytics

A team wants to analyze purchase behavior per customer in a separate analytics system.
The analysts do not need to know who the customers are, but they need to see that purchases belong to the same customer.

Review the options:

- Send the email address.
  This exposes direct identifiers to a wider group and violates minimization (8.3).
- Send a SHA-256 hash of the email address.
  Analysts, or anyone who obtains the data set, can hash a list of candidate email addresses and match them.
  Email addresses come from a small, guessable set, so this recovers customers (8.4).
- Send a salted hash with a unique salt per row.
  Purchases can no longer be linked to one customer, which defeats the goal.
- Send a salted hash with a single constant salt.
  Linking works, but the salt is not secret and enumeration still works.
- Send an HMAC of the email address with a secret key kept in the key management service, not in the analytics system.
  Linking works, and guessing candidates requires the key (8.2, 8.4).
  Alternatively, send a random token from a token vault controlled by the production system.

The team chooses the HMAC.
It also decides on these:

- The result is pseudonymized data, still personal data, so the analytics store gets access control, a retention period, and inclusion in the data inventory (8.1, 8.3).
- Deletion requests must reach the analytics system, either by deleting the person's rows or by deleting the mapping, so that no key-holder can link rows back (8.5).
- The pseudonym key is rotated only with a plan to re-pseudonymize existing rows.
- Tests confirm that raw email addresses appear in no analytics payload or log (8.8).
