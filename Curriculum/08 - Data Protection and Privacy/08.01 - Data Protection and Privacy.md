# Module 8 - Data Protection and Privacy

This outline defines the data protection and privacy knowledge assessed in Module 8.

## 8.1 Data classification and inventory

- Common classification levels: public, internal, confidential, and restricted
- Types of sensitive data: personal data, financial data, health data, credentials, and cryptographic keys
- Controls that depend on classification: access, encryption, storage location, sharing, retention, and disposal
- Places where sensitive data appears: databases, logs, caches, backups, search indexes, exports, analytics, and test environments
- The purpose of data flow maps and data inventories, and why they must be kept current
- The role and limits of automated data discovery tools
- Risks of improper data protection: leakage, unauthorized access, regulatory penalties, and harm to individuals

## 8.2 Protecting stored data and data in transit

- Differences between full disk encryption, database encryption (TDE), file-level encryption, and field-level encryption
- Which threats each storage encryption layer addresses and which it does not
- Why encryption at rest does not protect against a compromised application or an authorized database query
- Why keys must be stored separately from the data they protect
- Envelope encryption, key rotation, and managed key services (see Module 5, section 5.6)
- Effects of encryption on searching, indexing, and backups
- Why sensitive data must be protected in transit (see Module 9, sections 9.1 and 9.3)
- Unintended exposure of sensitive data in URLs, logs (see Module 7, section 7.2), caches, and client-side storage

## 8.3 Data minimization and retention

- Collecting only the data needed for a defined purpose
- Purpose limitation and why data is not reused for unrelated purposes
- Reducing copies of data, including replicas, exports, and test data
- Returning only the fields a client needs in API responses
- Defining and enforcing retention periods
- Keeping personal data out of logs and analytics

## 8.4 Anonymization and pseudonymization

- Difference between anonymized and pseudonymized data
- Why removing direct identifiers does not guarantee anonymity, and the role of quasi-identifiers
- Anonymization techniques: masking, generalization, swapping, and noise addition
- Pseudonymization techniques: tokenization, keyed hashing (HMAC), and encryption-based pseudonymization
- Why a plain or salted hash of a low-entropy identifier is not a safe pseudonym
- Reversible and irreversible techniques, and why pseudonymized data remains personal data
- Using masked, synthetic, or subset data in test environments instead of production data

## 8.5 Secure data deletion

- Deletion methods: cryptographic erasure, overwriting, and physical destruction
- Why cryptographic erasure is the primary method for modern and cloud storage
- Why overwriting is unreliable on solid-state drives and cloud storage
- Places where deleted data can persist: backups, caches, replicas, logs, and third-party processors
- Cascading deletion of related records
- Deletion requests from individuals and their verification
- Audit records of deletion that do not retain the deleted data

## 8.6 Privacy by design and by default

- Recognizing privacy by design and by default as design approaches, not a checklist
- Privacy impact assessments and when they are needed
- Privacy-protective default settings
- Consent records: what was agreed to, when, and how it is withdrawn
- Enforcing user privacy preferences in the processing paths they control

## 8.7 Privacy law and technical controls

- Applicable privacy law depends on where the individuals are and where the organization operates
- Examples of privacy laws: GDPR, UK GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, and LGPD
- Sector standards that affect data handling: HIPAA and PCI DSS
- GDPR terms: personal data, data subject, processing, controller, and processor
- GDPR lawful bases for processing: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, and legitimate interests
- GDPR principles and how they map to technical controls
- Individual rights: access, correction, erasure, restriction, portability, and objection
- Technical controls behind legal duties: access control, audit logging, breach detection, and deletion workflows
- Restrictions on transferring personal data across borders and the effect on data location

## 8.8 Verifying data protection controls

- Checking logs, error messages, API responses, URLs, and client-side storage for sensitive data
- Checking that sensitive data is actually encrypted in storage and in transit
- Checking that access to protected data is limited by role, object, and tenant
- Checking that deletion removes data from all stores
- Why automated scans and audits complement but do not replace these checks
